@front QUESTIONS FOR REFLECTION AND DISCUSSION
These questions can be used alone with a notebook, with a friend, or in a reading group. There are no correct answers. The useful ones are the honest ones.
### Part I — The Life You Are Sleepwalking Through
1. Which of your current routines began as a choice and now feels like a fact?
2. What have you been “preparing” for longer than a year? What would contact with reality look like?
3. The book distinguishes recovery from avoidance. Where in your week does one disguise itself as the other?
4. Which of the four voices—fear, ego, approval, values—most often gets the final word in your decisions?
5. What would you lose if you stopped being “someone who could” and became “someone who did,” even imperfectly?
### Part II — The War Inside You
1. Describe a time when changing your environment worked better than trying harder.
2. What small promise to yourself do you break most often? What would a smaller version you can keep be?
3. Is there a label you use about yourself that sounds like an observation but works like a prediction?
4. Who is in your invisible audience? What would change if they were no longer watching?
5. Which mountain in your life might not be yours? How would you find out?
6. What is one beginning you keep collecting instead of continuing?
### Part III — The People Who Refused to Remain Small
1. Alexander could not recognize enough. What would “enough” look like in one area of your life?
2. Musashi’s legend is cleaner than his history. What legend do you tell about yourself that the evidence does not fully support?
3. Jordan’s Pistons years suggest that sometimes the answer to failure is a better system, not more intensity. Where might that apply to you?
4. Ronaldo became better by changing the teenager he was. What part of your younger self are you protecting in the name of authenticity?
5. Frankl insisted that meaning is not a formula for survival. Why does that caution matter?
### Part IV — The Cage and the Open Door
1. When does looking at other people’s lives inform you, and when does it hand you a deadline you never agreed to?
2. What in your life are you defending simply because it is already assembled?
3. If the door opened tomorrow, what would you build on the other side—and what would your ordinary Tuesday look like there?
### Part V — What the Fire Is For
1. What are you giving your life to that you have never consciously chosen?
2. What is one sunk cost you might be protecting at the expense of the future?
3. After a goal you achieved, what happened on the day after the photograph?
4. If you knew you had five years left, what would you stop postponing?
5. The Epilogue says: “Burn. But not yourself.” What does that mean in your life right now?

@front A NOTE ON THE ILLUSTRATIONS
Every illustration and typographic plate in this edition was created for it, and they are designed to be read as one continuous story.
A single figure walks through this book: a nameless wanderer in a straw hat and a torn cloak, carrying a staff. You meet the wanderer on the opening page, climbing a slope beneath a wing too large to be real. You see the room with the unopened door in Chapter One, the reflection in the water at the start of Part II, the boulder on the hill, the first footprints in the snow, the river where an army refused to go farther, the cage that was never locked, and the fire that gives Part V its name. You see a terminal that keeps printing the same warning, a single tent by a river at night, a scoreboard over an empty arena, four shots that never reach the rim, one figure facing an army, a boulder finally set down at the top of the hill, and an old self burning into embers while the new one walks on. In the final chapter the same door opens, and the wanderer steps through it toward the mountain. The Epilogue ends beneath a burning star.
The wanderer has no face on purpose. It is not a hero to admire. It is a place for you to stand.
Three recurring symbols carry the book’s argument: the door (the choice you keep studying instead of making), the mountain (the goal, which keeps moving), and the fire (attention and care, which can warm or destroy). The style—black and white, high contrast, ink and grain—was chosen to keep attention on those symbols rather than on decoration.
The diagrams are numbered as figures and summarize ideas discussed in the text. They are simplifications meant to help you think, not substitutes for the research cited in the Notes.
While the book was being written, a private mood board of images collected online, many drawn from manga such as Takehiko Inoue’s Vagabond and Kentaro Miura’s Berserk, helped shape its atmosphere. Those images belong to their creators and are not reproduced here; the motifs they share are old ones, redrawn from scratch in this book’s own style.

@front ABOUT THE AUTHOR
Bazooka writes about the quiet kind of strength: the refusal to give up.
What inspires Bazooka is not overnight success or natural talent, but something far more ordinary and far more powerful—failing, learning, and trying again; making slow, consistent progress when fast progress is not available; and continuing to move toward a goal long after the excitement has faded.
*Burn to Become* grew out of that belief. It was written the same way it asks its readers to live: one honest step at a time, with more restarts than anyone will ever see.
