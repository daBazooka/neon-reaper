@front THE FIELD MANUAL | Thirty Days to Burn to Become | manual
A book can change how you see. Only behavior changes how you live.
This manual turns the ideas of the book into thirty short, concrete practices. Each takes between five and thirty minutes. None requires you to overhaul your life. Most ask you to notice something, write something down, or take one small action and then look honestly at what happened.
The thirty days are grouped into five weeks, one for each part of the book, plus a closing stretch. You do not have to do them on consecutive days. You do have to do them in writing. Thinking about an exercise and doing it are not the same thing, which is, in a sense, the argument of the entire book.
### Three rules before you begin
1. Accuracy over severity. When a question makes you uncomfortable, the goal is not the harshest answer. It is the most accurate one.
2. Smaller than you think. If a day’s action feels heroic, shrink it until it feels almost too easy. You are building evidence, not a performance.
3. Missing is not failing. If you miss a day, do not start over. Return. Returning is the skill.
A note of care: some of these practices touch on painful things—failure, loss, mortality, old wounds. If a question opens something that feels bigger than a notebook can hold, that is not weakness. It is a signal to talk to someone you trust or a qualified professional.

### Week One — See the Life You Are Living
@day 1 | The Receipts
Open your calendar, your screen-time report, and your bank statement for the last seven days. Without judging, write down the five things that received the most of your time, attention, and money.
Then write the five things you say matter most. Put the two lists side by side.
@day 2 | The Five-Year Projection
If nothing about your current habits changed for five years, describe the life they would build. Be specific: your body, your work, your relationships, your finances, your attention. Then circle the one sentence that bothers you most.
@day 3 | The Door
Name one door you have been studying instead of opening—an application, a conversation, a project, a question. Write what you fear is on the other side, and what might be. Then take one action that puts your hand on the handle: open the document, draft the email, book the time.
@day 4 | Recovery or Avoidance
Every time you take a break today, ask afterward: do I feel more able to return, or less? Keep a tally. At the end of the day, write down what the avoidance breaks were protecting you from.
@day 5 | Name the Fear
Choose the thing you have been postponing longest. Finish this sentence three different ways: “If I really try, I am afraid it will become true that…” Then finish this one once: “If I never try, it will become true that…”
@day 6 | Wish and Obstacle
Write one wish for the next three months. Write the best outcome in one sentence. Write the main inner obstacle. Then write a plan: “If [obstacle], then I will [action].”
@day 7 | Review
Reread the week’s pages. Answer one question: “What does my behavior seem to believe that my words do not?”

### Week Two — Confront the Patterns
@day 8 | The Friction Test
For one block of at least forty-five minutes, remove the easiest escape: phone in another room, one tab open, notifications off. Work on something that matters. Write down each urge to leave, what you wanted to escape to, and how long the urge lasted.
@day 9 | Your When–Then
Choose one behavior you want to build. Write: “When it is [time] and I am in [place], I will [first physical action].” Then write your minimum—the version you will still do on your worst ordinary day. Do the minimum today.
@day 10 | Redesign One Environment
Pick one place where you repeatedly make a choice you regret—your desk, your bed, your kitchen, your phone’s home screen. Make the better choice easier and the worse choice harder. Move one object. Delete one app from the home screen. Prepare one thing tonight for tomorrow morning.
@day 11 | One Small Promise
Make yourself one promise so small you cannot reasonably fail. Keep it. Write it in your evidence file (see below). Repeat this every day for the rest of the month.
@day 12 | Rewrite a Label
Write down one sentence you often say about yourself that sounds like a verdict: “I’m just not…”, “I always…”, “I’ve never been…”. Rewrite it as a practice: “I have practiced ___. I am now practicing ___.”
@day 13 | Translate Your Envy
Think of someone whose life you envy. Write what exactly you envy. Then ask underneath it: is it really their money, or security? Their body, or confidence? Their audience, or being seen? Write the need beneath the symbol, and one way to pursue that need directly.
@day 14 | Remove the Witness
Choose one current goal. Imagine that nobody will ever know you achieved it. No post, no announcement, no one impressed. Write honestly: do you still want it? If yes, why? If no, what were you really after?

### Week Three — Study Lives, Including Your Own
@day 15 | The Moving Summit
List three achievements from your past that you once badly wanted. For each, write how long the satisfaction lasted, and what goal replaced it. Ask Alexander’s question: what would “enough” look like for your current goal?
@day 16 | The Hidden Repetitions
Choose one skill you admire in someone else. Research, honestly, the unseen part: how many years, what instruction, what advantages, what setbacks. Then choose one skill of your own and schedule twenty minutes of deliberate practice aimed at a specific weakness.
@day 17 | A Better System, Not More Intensity
Recall a failure you responded to by “trying harder” that did not work. Write down what system, method, coach, or structure might have helped instead. Apply that thinking to one current problem.
@day 18 | Your Failure Portfolio
Write down three attempts that did not work. For each: what did you try, what happened, what did you learn, what would you change? Notice that you survived all three.
@day 19 | The Scoreboard Audit
List the numbers you check most often—followers, weight, grades, income, likes, rankings. For each, ask: is this information, or a verdict on my worth? Choose one to check less often this week.
@day 20 | What Is Life Asking?
Instead of asking what you want from life today, ask what today is asking of you. Write down the person, the responsibility, or the task in front of you that most needs your attention. Give it thirty undistracted minutes.
@day 21 | Review
Reread your Failure Portfolio and your Evidence File together. Write one paragraph describing the person the evidence suggests you are becoming.

### Week Four — Design the Life Beyond the Cage
@day 22 | Seven Days Without a Borrowed Deadline
Begin a one-week comparison log. Each time someone else’s life changes how you feel about your own, write what you saw and what you suddenly believed you were behind in. At the end of the week, answer: “If I had never seen another person’s progress, what would I still want?”
@day 23 | The Ten-Year Mirror
Imagine meeting yourself in ten years and discovering that almost nothing important changed. Write how you feel—peaceful, relieved, trapped? Then decide, in one sentence, whether the answer points toward leaving, staying more present, or simply appreciating what you have.
@day 24 | Design the Ordinary Tuesday
Fill in the worksheet below twice: once for your current Tuesday, and once for the Tuesday you want to be living in two years. Circle the three smallest differences that you could begin now.
@day 25 | One Experiment
Choose one thing worth trying next—a course, a conversation, a small version of a project, a month of living differently. Define it as an experiment: what will you do, for how long, and what information are you hoping to gain?
@day 26 | The Calendar as a Drawing
Open next week’s calendar. Put one block of time on it for each of the three things you say matter most. If nothing on the calendar belongs to what matters, that absence is today’s finding.

### Week Five — Know What the Fire Is For
@day 27 | What Are You Willing to Lose?
Write one thing you are pursuing. Then write the honest price: what it costs in time, money, relationships, comfort, and other possibilities. Ask: if I were starting today, knowing what I know now, would I choose this again?
@day 28 | Control, Influence, Accept
Pick your biggest current worry. Divide a page into three columns: what I can control, what I can influence, what I cannot control. Take one action from the first column today. Write one sentence of acceptance for the third.
@day 29 | A Letter to the Old You
Write to the person you were five years ago. Thank them for what they carried. Tell them what they misunderstood. Tell them what you are keeping and what you are leaving behind.
@day 30 | The Last Ordinary Day
Imagine this is your last ordinary day. Write down who you would call, what you would stop caring about, and what you would finally do. Then choose one item from the list and do it today—not recklessly, but deliberately.
### After the thirty days
Do not start over. Continue.
Keep the evidence file. Keep one small promise a day. Once a month, redo Day 1 and Day 24. Once a year, redo Day 2 and Day 30.
And every morning, before the phone and before the noise, ask the question from the last chapter:
> What is one thing I already know I need to do?
Then do it.
